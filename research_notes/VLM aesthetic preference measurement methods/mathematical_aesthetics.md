## 1. Balance: formal definitions (centre of mass, Arnheim, APB, DCM, homogeneity, symmetry) and recent human evidence

### Takeaway
Pictorial balance is almost always formalised as the distance between a "visual centre of mass" and the frame centre. In that model each pixel's weight is its darkness (DCM), or the weight is split into eight half-versus-half pixel-count differences (APB). On simple black-and-white grids, these scores correlate strongly with mean liking across pictures (|r| about .74-.87) but only modestly within a single person (|r| about .28-.46). Liking for these pictures is predicted about as well by homogeneity (how evenly elements are spread) as by balance. Recent work (2019-2026) shows the balance-liking link is fragile. It depends on the evaluation method (rating vs producing), on picture type (single vs multiple elements, simple vs complex), and on vertical "stability" as against left-right balance. For two-object displays, the literal physical lever model has failed outright.

### Cited Findings

**Formal definitions (from Hübner & Fillinger 2016, Frontiers in Psychology [peer-reviewed])**
- **Visual weight.** For black-and-white pictures, each black pixel has mass 1 and each white pixel mass 0. McManus et al. (2011) instead give each pixel a mass equal to its darkness (inverse grey level). — [Hübner & Fillinger 2016, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786554/)
- **APB (Assessment of Preference for Balance; Wilson & Chatterjee 2005 [classic]), as Hübner & Fillinger restate it.** Let N be the total number of black pixels. Split the picture into four strips A1-A4 along an axis (and the same for the other axes). The horizontal component is h = |(A1+A2) − (A3+A4)| / N × 100. The horizontal inner-outer component is h_io = |(A1+A4) − (A2+A3)| / N × 100. The vertical (v, v_io), main-diagonal (md, md_io) and anti-diagonal (ad, ad_io) components are built the same way. APB is the mean of these 8 components, and **lower APB means more balanced**. — [Hübner & Fillinger 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786554/)
- **DCM (Deviation of the Centre of "Mass"; Hübner & Fillinger 2016).** Let m_i be the mass in column i and r_i its position. Then b_x = Σ m_i r_i / Σ m_i, normalised as b′_x = b_x / w (and b′_y the same way). With d_x = 0.5 − b′_x and d_y = 0.5 − b′_y, **DCM = 100 · √(d_x² + d_y²) / 0.5**, and higher DCM means less balanced. This is explicitly an operationalisation of Arnheim's balance idea. — [Hübner & Fillinger 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786554/)
- **Homogeneity (HG).** The picture is binned into 10 bins per dimension. The relative entropy of each marginal is E_r = −Σ p_i ln p_i / ln 10, and HG = 100 × the mean of E_rx and E_ry (higher means more evenly spread). **Mirror symmetry (MS)** follows Bauerly & Liu (2006): pixel-match counts, weighted by distance from each of 4 axes, averaged across the axes. (The source's rendering of the MS and HG formulas was partly garbled, so these two are reconstructed from context.) — [Hübner & Fillinger 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786554/)

**Effect sizes on simple 7-element grids (Hübner & Fillinger 2016 [peer-reviewed])**
- **Experiment 1** (N=16, 130 APB pictures, balance ratings). Across-picture correlations with mean balance ratings: APB r = −.784, DCM r = −.822, HG r = .707, MS r = .418. Mean per-participant correlations with balance: APB about −.45 to −.48, DCM about −.48 to −.49. Rated symmetry correlated .929 with rated balance. — [Hübner & Fillinger 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786554/)
- **Experiment 2** (N=21, liking). Across-picture correlations with mean liking: APB r = −.867, DCM r = −.844, **HG r = .848**, MS r = .199. Mean per-participant correlations with liking: APB −.423 / −.459, DCM −.401 / −.438, HG .408 / .441, MS .084 / .127. APB correlated with liking significantly more strongly than DCM did. Liking correlated .816 with rated balance and .900 with rated symmetry. — [Hübner & Fillinger 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786554/)
- **Experiment 3** (N=23, new pictures with Gaussian-placed elements). Across-picture correlations with mean balance: DCM r = −.916, APB r = −.836. With mean liking: APB −.737, DCM −.742, HG .697. Per-participant correlations with liking were small and did not differ between measures: **APB −.276, DCM −.292, HG −.265**. — [Hübner & Fillinger 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786554/)
- **Authors' conclusions.** DCM is the better predictor of *perceived balance*. APB is equal or better for *preference* because its inner-outer components partly measure homogeneity, and HG "could be used as a simpler substitute". Pixel mirror symmetry is the weakest predictor. The results depend on the stimulus set (circles were liked more than hexagons in every experiment). — [Hübner & Fillinger 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786554/)
- **Component weighting.** In APB, the horizontal (left vs right) component dominated the regression (β ≈ −.23). Weighting the eight components instead of averaging them raised R² for balance from .615 to .751. — [Hübner & Fillinger 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786554/)

**Arnheim's structural skeleton and the literal centre-of-mass (lever) model [peer-reviewed; McManus, Stöver & Kim 2011, i-Perception, the main test of Arnheim's theory]**
- **Centre of mass.** R = Σ m_i r_i / Σ m_i, with pixel mass = darkness (0-255). An image counts as "on axis" if its CoM lies within ±0.5% of a horizontal, vertical or diagonal axis. Results held when pixel mass was raised to γ = 0.5, 1 or 2 and when distance was raised to the power 0.5, 1 or 2. In other words, the conclusions are not specific to a linear weight × distance law. — [McManus, Stöver & Kim 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485801)
- **Art photographs** (455, against 595-597 random photos). 46.6% of art photos had their CoM on an axis, against 39.7% of random photos (χ² = 5.02, p = .028). Distance to the image centre was .0444 vs .0516 (p < .001). The vertical axis dominated for art photos (47.7%, vs 36.9% for random photos). The authors call this "not a strong test" of the theory. — [McManus et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485801)
- **Preference studies** (Studies 2 and 4). There was **no preference for images with the CoM on an axis**; the on-axis version was in fact chosen *less* often (42.6%, p = .00074). Experts and non-experts did not differ. — [McManus et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485801)
- **Two-disc framing** (Study 5; N=36, 24 images; participants moved a frame around two discs; **directly relevant to E2**). The lever model predicts that the frame centre should move toward the heavier (darker or larger) disc. Instead, participants kept the frame **midway between the discs in almost every condition**. Changing disc size, greyness or background "has no effect at all upon their framing" (Friedman tests mostly non-significant, e.g. grey vs black disc p = .89 / .77). The authors call this "a paradox" and conclude that the physicalist reading of Arnheim and Ross is not supported. — [McManus et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485801)

**Recent work (2019-2026): balance depends on picture type, evaluation method and the vertical axis**
- **Hübner & Fillinger 2019** (i-Perception) [peer-reviewed; abstract-level snippet only]. Single-element pictures are judged by "mechanical" balance. Multi-element and dynamic-pattern pictures are judged more as *gravitational stability*. The relation between balance, stability and appreciation "depend[s] on the picture type". — [Hübner & Fillinger 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2041669519856040)
- **Hübner 2025** (i-Perception 16(6)) [peer-reviewed, open access; I could read only the abstract/record]. Experiment 1: when participants **rated precomposed pictures**, they preferred (positional) **symmetry over balance, and balance over proximity**. Experiment 2 was a **production task** with movable elements: participants frequently used **proximity**, with some tendency toward positional symmetry. The author concludes that the assessment method substantially changes which composition rules are preferred. The proposed reason is that rating encourages a global focus (favouring symmetry and balance) while producing encourages a local focus (favouring proximity). — [Hübner 2025, KOPS record](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/0343e5f2-5f99-44a8-8ce4-0d35c6ed9b29); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/20416695251381548)
- **Braun, Hofmann & Doerschner 2026** ("Being K. Malevich", i-Perception 17(1)) [peer-reviewed].
  - Setup: 21 art-naïve adults arranged Suprematist shapes (2-8 per composition) to look "stable" or "dynamic" and rated their own and the original compositions on 0-1 sliders.
  - **APB and DCM tracked perceived balance and stability but did not correlate with liking** for self-made compositions. For the 10 originals, liking was unrelated to balance and to the geometric indices, and correlations varied with complexity, sometimes changing sign.
  - Self-made *dynamic* compositions were liked most (.595 vs .521 stable vs .481 originals).
  - The *dynamic* compositions had **lower** DCM (24.96 vs 41.21) and APB (35.72 vs 42.83) than the stable ones (p < .0001). That is, the indices rated them as *more* balanced.
  - The abstract states that the geometric balance indices "did not correlate strongly with perceptual ratings and liking". The authors note earlier failures of APB/DCM on complex stimuli such as Japanese calligraphy (Fillinger & Hübner 2020) and suggest the indices capture "mechanical" rather than perceived balance. Individual differences in liking were large.
  - — [Braun et al. 2026, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12909777)
- **Luminance-quadrant "balance" model** [preprint, bioRxiv, many revisions to v18]. Proposes balance as a centre-of-quadrant-luminance equation analogous to centre of mass, mediated by eye movements. Its status is unclear, and I found no peer-reviewed version. — [bioRxiv 2020.05.26.104687v18](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.05.26.104687v18)
- **Colour images.** One study computed balance from a "visual centre of gravity" of colour images, in which colour supplies the weight and shape the occupied space, and treated an image as balanced when its visual CoG coincides with the geometric centre [peer-reviewed, 2016; abstract-level]. — [Parada-Castellano et al. 2016, Color Res. Appl.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/col.21943)

### Inferences
- **Functional form for E1.** Every validated measure is a monotone *distance* (L1 differences for APB, L2 CoM offset for DCM). No study I found tests a non-monotone (inverted-U) dose-response for balance. The default prediction is therefore monotone: preference falls as APB or DCM rises. A Thurstone-style translation of the rating correlations into two-alternative choice (my derivation, not from a source) is P(choose the more balanced picture) = Φ(ρ·Δ / √(2(1−ρ²))), where Δ is the score difference in SD units:
  - With the per-participant ρ ≈ .28-.46 (Hübner & Fillinger 2016), Δ = 1 SD gives P ≈ .58-.64 and Δ = 2 SD gives P ≈ .66-.77.
  - With the across-picture ρ ≈ .75-.87 that applies to *averaged* raters, Δ = 1 SD gives P ≈ .79-.89.
  - So human-like model choices should rise smoothly with the pair's score difference and stay near .5 for small differences. A model choosing the balanced image near-deterministically even at small Δ would be "more balance-driven than humans".
- **Confound for E1.** Because APB's inner-outer components partly measure homogeneity, and homogeneity predicts liking as well as APB does, an E1 effect scored with APB cannot be attributed to centre-of-mass balance alone. Computing DCM and HG on the same stimuli (and pixel mirror symmetry, which is a weak predictor in humans) would let model choices be regressed on each measure separately. In humans, the horizontal (left-right) component carries most weight, so a model that is sensitive mainly to vertical CoM shifts would be non-human-like.
- **E2 (two objects) is expected to be weak or null in humans.** The best direct test of the lever model (McManus et al. 2011) found people ignore size and greyness differences when framing two discs, and the null held across power-law variants of weight and distance. This fits the project's human values of .43-.56. A strong model preference for the "physically balanced" two-object layout would therefore reflect textbook knowledge of design rules ("heavier object nearer the fulcrum") more than human perception.
- **Vertical stability vs balance.** The Braun et al. result (stacked "stable" compositions get *worse* DCM) and Hübner & Fillinger 2019 (multi-element pictures judged by gravitational stability) suggest a vertical CoM below centre is not perceived as imbalance. A symmetric L2 DCM therefore misdescribes human perception along the vertical axis.
- **Method dependence matters for interpretation.** Hübner 2025 suggests evaluative tasks on finished pictures (such as two-alternative choice between finished images) favour symmetry and balance more than production tasks do. The project's choice paradigm sits on the evaluative side, so the human benchmark is the "rating" pattern (symmetry > balance > proximity).

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the full text of Hübner 2025: sample sizes, test statistics and effect sizes are missing, as is any comparison of rating vs pairwise choice (Sage returned 403; the PDF could not be parsed here).
- I found no 2019-2026 *direct replication* of Wilson & Chatterjee (2005) with the original APB stimuli, and no test of the dose-response *shape* (linear vs saturating) of balance preference.
- I could not verify the full details of the Hübner & Fillinger 2019 i-Perception paper beyond the abstract snippet.
- A 2024 EEG study in *Symmetry* (Lin et al.) reportedly found that participants used balance features (symmetry, centre of gravity, negative space). I saw it only as a search summary with no reachable URL, so it is not used.

## 2. Information-theoretic and computational models of aesthetic value (Birkhoff, entropy and complexity, fluency, predictive processing, Brielmann & Dayan, Schmidhuber) and what they predict for the five effects

### Takeaway
Formal models fall into three families:
- **Static order/complexity measures** (Birkhoff M = O/C and entropy or compression descendants).
- **Likelihood/fluency models**, in which value is the log-probability of a stimulus under the observer's internal model.
- **Learning-progress models**, in which value is the *change* in the internal model's fit (Schmidhuber compression progress; Brielmann & Dayan's ΔV term; active-inference accounts).

Empirically, order and complexity contribute *additively* rather than as Birkhoff's ratio. Brielmann & Dayan's model fits individual ratings well (median trial-level r ≈ .65). None of these models makes balance, inward facing or colour weight predictions directly. They do so only through what the observer's learned priors say about typical images.

### Cited Findings
- **Birkhoff's measure [classic].** M = O/C, where O is order (regularity, symmetry, harmony) and C is complexity (variety, unpredictability, entropy). It is the root of later order-complexity models. — [Emergent Mind topic summary (secondary)](https://api.emergentmind.com/topics/order-complexity-aesthetic-assessment-model)
- **A known pathology of the O/C ratio.** A Birkhoff-style measure applied to modified images "showed a preference for the least complex of the images". An entropy-only measure failed to discriminate. A compressibility-based variant gave qualitatively the same results as the traditional one [preprint, Preprints.org 2018]. — [Preprints.org 201801.0098](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/201801.0098/v1)
- **Compression-based complexity.** JPEG compression ratio correlates with perceived complexity of abstract artistic images (r_s = .51, an earlier result cited in this paper). The paper applies a battery of complexity measures to three evolutionary-art datasets and examines their correlation with individual aesthetic judgement [peer-reviewed, Genetic Programming and Evolvable Machines 2022]. — [McCormack & Gambardella 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10710-022-09429-9)
- **Order and complexity are additive, not a ratio** [peer-reviewed; Van Geert & Wagemans 2021, PACA 15(3), 484-504].
  - Design: 421 participants made paired choices on 100 image pairs of neatly organised compositions; 84 also rated 184 images.
  - Result: subjective order and subjective complexity were related to appreciation "independently", and "the balance between order and complexity involves no interaction". High order with high complexity was rated "fascinating"; high order with low complexity was rated "soothing". Participants differed in which they preferred, partly explained by age and personality.
  - — [Van Geert & Wagemans 2021 (author page)](https://elinevg.github.io/publications/2021_PACA_NeatlyOrganized.html)
- **Review** [peer-reviewed; Van Geert & Wagemans 2020, PACA]. Order is the structure and organisation of information; complexity is its quantity and variety. The review notes mixed support for the classic inverted-U for complexity. — [Van Geert & Wagemans 2020](https://elinevg.github.io/publications/2020_PACA_ReviewOrdCompl.html)
- **Aesthetic Quality Model** [peer-reviewed; Krpan & van Tilburg 2022]. Argues that the complexity-beauty relation depends on a third property, *randomness*, and notes that the inverted-U view has produced mixed results. — [Krpan & van Tilburg 2022 (PDF)](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/novelty/2022-krpan.pdf)
- **Brielmann & Dayan 2022, "A computational model of aesthetic value"** (Psychological Review 129(6), 1319-1337) [peer-reviewed; abstract-level].
  - Value has two parts: (i) **immediate sensory reward**, defined as processing fluency and "quantified by the likelihood of that stimulus given an observer's state"; and (ii) **the change in expected future reward** (learning).
  - The model "melds processing fluency theories (immediate reward) and learning theories (change in expected future reward)".
  - A simple version accounts for empirical effects of **exposure, complexity and symmetry**.
  - Code: github.com/aenneb/intro-aesthetic-value-model.
  - — [Tübingen repository record](https://tobias-lib.ub.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/141335); [DOI landing](https://zendy.io/title/10.1037/rev0000337)
- **Formal structure, as restated in the 2023 follow-up (Brielmann, Berentelg & Dayan, Phil Trans B 379: 20220414)** [peer-reviewed].
  - System state X is an n-dimensional Gaussian over stimulus features.
  - Immediate reward **r(t) = log p(stimulus features | X)**.
  - The expected true distribution p_T is a second Gaussian. Learning moves the mean of X toward each stimulus at rate α.
  - The **learning value ΔV(t)** is the change in the distance between X and p_T caused by that update.
  - Aesthetic value **A(t) = w_r·r(t) + w_V·ΔV(t) + bias**, with 2n + 6 free parameters.
  - Fit: on 55 VGG-16-feature dog morphs (PCA to 2-3 dimensions), 59 MTurk raters, individually fitted with 16-fold cross-validation. The full 3-D model reached **median trial-level r = .65**, against .01 for the population-average baseline; median test RMSE was .15 vs .20. The true trial order explained about 17% more variance than shuffled orders.
  - — [Brielmann, Berentelg & Dayan 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10725758)
- **Further use of the model** [preprint, bioRxiv 2024]. A decision-theoretic model of multistability uses Brielmann & Dayan's value as its aesthetic reward term, deriving aesthetic value from "optimising a sensory system to process present and future stimuli well". — [bioRxiv 2024.12.06.627286](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.12.06.627286v1.full)
- **Active inference / predictive processing** [peer-reviewed; Van de Cruys, Frascaroli & Friston 2024, Phil Trans B 379: 20220411; abstract-level, since the PMC full text was behind a reCAPTCHA]. Proposes an "epistemic arc" (curiosity, then epistemic action, then an "aha" experience) as aspects of active inference. The account covers both uncertainty-reducing closure and open-ended, playful exploration. — [PMC record](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10725768/); [PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/rec/VANOAC-4)
- **Related chapter** (Van de Cruys, Bervoets & Moors 2022, Routledge Handbook of Neuroaesthetics) [peer-reviewed chapter]. "Preferences need inferences": links learning, valuation and curiosity, with a focus on non-reinforced preference. — [KU Leuven PDF](https://fac.ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/VandeCruys2022PNILV.pdf)
- **Schmidhuber compression progress [classic, arXiv 2008/2009].** Subjective beauty tracks the momentary compressibility of the data given the observer's compressor. Interestingness and curiosity track the *first derivative*: compression progress, i.e. learning progress. — [Schmidhuber, arXiv:0812.4360](https://arxiv.org/abs/0812.4360)

### Inferences (theory-derived predictions; none of these models was directly tested on E1-E5 stimuli)
- **Balance (E1, E2).** Static O/C and compression models predict a preference for balance only to the extent that balance raises regularity or compressibility. Mirror-symmetric layouts compress better; a merely CoM-centred but irregular layout does not. These models therefore predict *symmetry* effects more than pure CoM-balance effects, consistent with the human finding that symmetry > balance in rating (Hübner 2025) and that rated symmetry tracks liking (r = .90; Hübner & Fillinger 2016). Fluency or likelihood models (the r(t) term) predict a balance preference if the observer's prior over compositions is centred on balanced or axial layouts. Real art photographs do show CoM near the vertical axis more often than chance (McManus et al. 2011), which would support such a prior.
- **Additive vs ratio.** The human evidence (Van Geert & Wagemans 2021) favours additive order and complexity effects. If a project manipulates balance while holding complexity constant, an additive model predicts a balance effect of the same size at any complexity level. Birkhoff's ratio predicts a larger balance effect at low complexity.
- **Inward bias (E3).** The predictive-processing reading (see Q3: Chen et al. 2018, "witness predicted future actions") is that more frame space where the object's predicted future lies yields a higher expected reward or epistemic value. This predicts a dependence on the *ratio* of front space to back space rather than on absolute position.
- **Colour weight (E4) and proportion (E5).** None of the formal value models makes a principled prediction for colour weight or for golden-section proportions. Likelihood-based models predict preference for proportions that are frequent in the observer's experience (e.g. 4:3, 3:2, square), not for 1.618 as such.
- **History dependence.** The Brielmann & Dayan learning term implies that preference depends on the preceding stimulus sequence (the true order explained about 17% more variance). For humans, trial order and blocking of balanced vs unbalanced stimuli are therefore not neutral.

### Gaps
- I could not access the full Brielmann & Dayan 2022 text to quote its exact symmetry simulation (how symmetric stimuli are represented in feature space).
- I found no study fitting Birkhoff O/C, compression progress or the Brielmann & Dayan model to balance, inward-bias, colour-weight or golden-ratio stimuli specifically.
- The Van de Cruys et al. 2024 full text was not retrievable (reCAPTCHA), so I cannot report any formal equation (e.g. valence as a rate of free-energy reduction) from it.

## 3. Spatial composition: centre bias, inward bias, facing direction, position preferences

### Takeaway
The centre bias and the inward bias (Palmer and colleagues) are among the most replicated effects in compositional aesthetics. The best-supported quantitative account (Forman, Chen, Scholl & Alvarez 2021) is that people keep a roughly constant *ratio* of space in front of versus behind a facing object across frame widths. This is a proportional "affordance space" rule, not a fixed offset from centre. Gaze alone suffices to produce the bias. Semantic context (titles) can change or reverse it. Reading direction can shift left-right preferences, but the cross-cultural evidence is thin and old.

### Cited Findings
- **Palmer, Gardner & Wickens 2008 [classic; peer-reviewed, Spatial Vision].** Two main biases in framing single objects: a **centre bias** (preference for placement near the frame centre, strongest for front-facing or non-directional objects) and an **inward bias** (objects facing into the frame preferred over those facing out). — [Palmer, Gardner & Wickens 2008 (PDF)](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/PalmerGardner&Wickens-1.pdf)
- **Implied motion and facing direction.** Found a "strong inward bias: right-facing objects were preferred left-of-center" (Palmer-lab related work; the snippet does not identify the paper). — [Effects of Implied Motion and Facing Direction on Positional Preferences (PDF)](https://www.thomaslanglois.net/files/Effects-of-Implied-Motion-and-Facing-Direction-on-Positional-Preferences-in-Single-Object-Pictures.pdf)
- **Multi-object groups.** The inward bias replicates for the "spatial envelope" of multi-object groups [peer-reviewed; Leyssen et al. 2012, i-Perception]. — [Leyssen et al. 2012 (PDF)](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/Leyssen.i-perception.2012.pdf)
- **Semantic context** [peer-reviewed; Sammartino & Palmer 2012]. The "seemingly robust" centre and inward biases could be manipulated by adding titles that changed the image's metaphorical meaning. — [Sammartino & Palmer 2012 (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236182916_Aesthetic_Issues_in_Spatial_Composition_Representational_Fit_and_the_Role_of_Semantic_Context)
- **Perception, not only preference** [peer-reviewed]. Ambiguous figures placed near a frame border are preferentially perceived as the version "looking" inward. — ["Seeing and liking" (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261221856_Seeing_and_liking_Biased_perception_of_ambiguous_figures_consistent_with_the_inward_bias_in_aesthetic_preferences)
- **Gaze alone** [peer-reviewed; Chen, Colombatto & Scholl 2018, Cognition 176: 209-214]. People with direct gaze were not placed preferentially in any region. People with *averted gaze* were reliably placed so that they appeared to look inward. The authors propose the bias reflects a preference for viewpoints that let us witness *predicted future actions*, not global shape or salient features. — [Yale demo / summary page](https://perception.yale.edu/Brian/demos/Aesthetics-InwardBiasGaze.html); [scispace record](https://scispace.com/papers/looking-into-the-future-an-inward-bias-in-aesthetic-3zzhz7s7sh)
- **Frame width: the main quantitative model** [peer-reviewed; Forman, Chen, Scholl & Alvarez 2021, Attention, Perception & Psychophysics 83(5): 2151-2158; abstract-level]. Observers placed a profile face in frames of different widths. They showed a strong inward preference, and **the ratio of space in front of versus behind the face stayed roughly constant across frame widths**. The authors read this as evidence that the bias is anchored to the frame's borders and predicted future action, not to centring ("The center cannot hold"). — [MEDLINE record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/bvsms/resource/pt/mdl-33811279); [author PDF](https://perception.yale.edu/papers/21-Forman-EtAl-APP.pdf)
- **Reading direction and culture** [peer-reviewed; 1999, Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology; abstract-level]. Arabic and Hebrew readers preferred right-turning facial and bodily profiles; Russian readers preferred left-turning ones. The authors attribute this to reading and writing habits rather than brain laterality. — [DOI 10.1177/0022022199030001006](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022199030001006); [Scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/8d6bf3c92e204a42245d1c62c59d61c2)
- **Review of reading-direction effects** [peer-reviewed; Frontiers in Psychology 2021]. Left-to-right readers reliably show a leftward bias; right-to-left readers show a central or rightward bias in several visuospatial tasks. Hebrew findings are mixed and samples are small. — [Frontiers 2021 (PDF)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.654266/pdf)

### Inferences
- **Mathematical form for E3.** Let the frame have width W and the object sit at horizontal position x, facing direction s ∈ {+1, −1}. Front space is F = W/2 − s·x (relative to centre) and back space is B = W/2 + s·x. Forman et al. imply that the preferred position satisfies **F/B ≈ k** for some constant k > 1, which gives x* = −s · (W/2) · (k−1)/(k+1). The preferred offset therefore **scales linearly with frame width**: it is a proportional rule, not a fixed number of pixels.
- **Consequences for a two-alternative choice between inward and outward versions at offset d from centre.** At d = 0 the two are identical in F/B, so P(inward) ≈ .5. As d grows, the outward version's F/B ratio falls quickly (less space in front), so P(inward) should rise monotonically with |d|. This matches Palmer et al.'s .70 → .82 increase across the two offsets in the project brief.
- **Centre vs quarter line.** For front-facing or non-directional objects, the centre bias predicts centre > quarter line. For a profile facing inward, the F/B rule predicts that an off-centre position behind centre can match or beat dead centre, depending on k. With k ≈ 2 (an illustrative value only; the abstract does not give k), x* is about −W/6, close to the third line. Centre-vs-quarter results therefore need to be interpreted conditional on facing direction.
- **Directional cues.** Chen et al. (2018) suggest a model's E3 behaviour depends on whether the generated abstract objects carry a recognisable "front" (gaze, motion, pointing). Abstract shapes with weak directionality should show attenuated inward bias in humans too.
- **Reading direction** could add a left-right asymmetry: inward-facing preference might differ for left-facing vs right-facing objects. Effects are culture-dependent and should be checked by splitting results by facing direction.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve full-text numbers for Forman et al. 2021 (the value of the constant front/back ratio, N, frame widths) or for Chen et al. 2018 (N, proportions). The publisher pages refused the fetch, and the PDF was binary.
- I found no 2022-2026 replication of Palmer, Gardner & Wickens's exact single-object paradigm with large online samples, and no formal cross-cultural replication of the inward bias itself (as opposed to left/right profile preference).
- I found no mathematical model of the *vertical* position preference (e.g. the vertical work in Palmer's lab), beyond the existence of the academia.edu record on vertical position and perspective.

## 4. Colour and perceived visual weight; golden-ratio and proportion preferences

### Takeaway
Colour weight is the least-studied effect in 2019-2026. The settled older consensus is that darker colours look heavier and hue matters less. Red and blue usually rank heavier than yellow, but the size of the difference depends on area (Locher et al. 2005) and on method. For proportion, recent large-sample evidence finds **no golden-ratio peak**: preference for rectangles peaks at the square, with wide shapes slightly preferred to tall ones. Individual differences are large and stable (McManus). E5 is correctly treated as an unanchored control.

### Cited Findings

**Colour weight**
- **Locher, Overbeeke & Stappers 2005** (Perception 34(2): 169-189) [peer-reviewed; abstract-level]. The three primary-colour areas in six Mondrian compositions were recoloured into all 6 arrangements (36 stimuli). Participants located each composition's balance centre and rated the heaviness of each colour area. The perceived weight of a colour, **especially red and yellow, varied with the size of the area it occupied**. Only design-trained participants detected significant shifts in balance centre across the variations. — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:666504ef-d049-40d1-a1bc-3f52c1f9cf81); [scite abstract](https://scite.ai/authors/pj-locher-pxN5kw)
- **Original Mondrians** were judged balanced near the centre; recoloured versions were generally seen as less balanced (as described in a later review) [peer-reviewed]. — [PMC3485813](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485813/)
- **Older heaviness work** [peer-reviewed, older]. Red and blue were consistently perceived as heaviest and yellow as lightest, with brightness controlled. The consensus is that darker objects appear heavier. Some summaries say hue has little influence once lightness is controlled, so the hue ranking is partly a lightness effect. — ["The apparent heaviness of colors" (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/18325818_The_apparent_heaviness_of_colors)
- **Colour weight photometry** (Vision Research, about 2017) [peer-reviewed; abstract-level]. Equating colour "weight" on displays is method-dependent. The closest balance between display primaries is about R:G:B = 1:1:1, and equal-luminance comparisons can fail (a dim "yellow" looks drab brown rather than yellow). — [ScienceDirect S0042698917301244](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0042698917301244)

**Proportion and the golden ratio**
- **Lee, Van Geert, Çelen, Marjieh, van Rijn, Park & Jacoby 2025** ("Visual and Auditory Aesthetic Preferences Across Cultures", arXiv:2502.14439v2) [preprint; a Cognitive Science Society proceedings version appears to exist but I did not verify it].
  - Design: 4,835 participants in 10 countries gave 401,403 judgements. Rectangles had sides of 20-60 px, aspect ratios from 1:1 to 1:3, and were rated for pleasantness on a 7-point scale.
  - **Preference peaked at 1:1** and declined with elongation. There was **no peak at 1.618:1 or its inverse in any country**.
  - Wide rectangles were preferred over equally elongated tall ones (z = .27 vs .17; t = 76.5). South Korea showed peaks near 4:3 and 3:2. Between-country agreement was high (ρ = .84-.89).
  - There was also a consistent cross-cultural preference for symmetric forms.
  - — [arXiv:2502.14439v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14439v2)
- **McManus (1980; McManus, Cook & Hunt 2010, PACA) [classic/peer-reviewed; as summarised in a 2016 Frontiers paper].** Paired comparisons of rectangles showed a **weak group-level golden-section preference** but **strong individual preference functions, stable over time**. A Q-mode factor analysis found two factors (near-square vs elongated). These were not explained by the Big Five or other trait measures. — [Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 2016](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2016.00350/full)
- **"Time to let go?"** [peer-reviewed; Stieger & Swami 2015]. Found no automatic preference for the golden ratio in art pictures across several countries; preference peaked at the 1:1 square. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/276350506_Time_to_let_go_No_automatic_aesthetic_preference_for_the_golden_ratio_in_art_pictures)
- **Fechner's original data and range artifacts.** In Fechner's own data the golden rectangle reportedly ranked only 7th of 10 (Green 1995 review [classic]). Godkewitsch (1974) argued that the apparent golden-ratio preference was an artifact of the golden rectangle's position within the stimulus range [classic]. — [Green 1995 (PDF)](https://www.yorku.ca/christo/papers/Green.golden.Perception-1995.pdf); [Plus Magazine](https://plus.maths.org/golden-ratio-and-aesthetics)
- **Culture.** A Korean sample (N = 200) favoured the root-2 rectangle (1:1.414) over the golden ratio [peer-reviewed, older]. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/274875871_Comparison_between_rectangular_proportions_Golden_ratio_versus_Root_ratio)

### Inferences
- **E4 functional form.** A linear mass model (weight = darkness × area, as in DCM or McManus's CoM) predicts that red, being darker than yellow, is heavier at *every* area. The ratio of their weights would be fixed (the ratio of their darknesses), and the red-heavier effect in a balance task would grow linearly with area. Locher's finding (red > yellow at small and medium area, but not at large) implies **a colour × area interaction that the linear model cannot produce**. Perceived weight has to saturate with area, or hue/saturation weight has to fall off at large areas. A model that reproduces "red heavier" at all sizes is following a darkness-based mass rule, not the human pattern.
- **Lightness confound.** Because lightness is the dominant driver of perceived weight, an E4 red-vs-yellow difference cannot be attributed to hue unless lightness is equated. Even equal-luminance comparisons have problems (colour weight photometry).
- **E5 expected human baseline.** For golden ratio vs another ratio, population P is likely near .5. Where it departs from .5, it should favour whichever option is closer to square (or wider rather than taller) and depend on the stimulus range. A model showing a strong golden-ratio preference is reproducing a cultural myth rather than measured human preference.

### Gaps
- I found **no 2019-2026 replication of Locher et al. 2005**, and no recent experiment on red/yellow/blue visual weight across area in balance tasks. Effect sizes for the area interaction could not be retrieved (I had only the abstract).
- I found no formal meta-analysis of golden-ratio preference. The large 2025 preprint is the best recent evidence, but it is not yet confirmed as peer-reviewed.
- I did not verify claims in non-scholarly sources (e.g. a photography blog's "2.1× red vs blue" weight ratio), so they are excluded.

## 5. Computational measures applied to images and neural-network predictors: do they encode balance and composition?

### Takeaway
Standardised tools now compute balance, symmetry and entropy measures on arbitrary images (Redies et al.'s Aesthetics Toolbox, 2024-2025). Deep networks trained only for object recognition explain much of the variance in average beauty ratings, and features such as the vertical position of the largest segment's centre of mass emerge in deeper layers. However, explicit "balancing elements" and "rule of thirds" attributes are among the hardest for CNN aesthetic predictors, and probing work suggests CLIP lacks a robust model of aesthetic form. No study I found directly tests whether CLIP or VLM embeddings encode APB/DCM balance.

### Cited Findings
- **Aesthetics Toolbox** (Redies, Bartho, Koßmann, Spehar, Hübner, Wagemans & Hayn-Leichsenring) [preprint arXiv:2408.10616, 2024; reported as published in Behavior Research Methods 2025, 57: 117, DOI 10.3758/s13428-025-02632-3].
  - Open-source Python 3 port of four groups' scripts, with outputs checked against the originals.
  - Computes lightness and colour statistics, Fourier spectral slope, fractality, self-similarity, symmetry, several entropy measures (e.g. edge-orientation entropy) and CNN-based variances.
  - Code at github.com/RBartho/Aesthetics-Toolbox. The abstract does not confirm whether balance (DCM) is included, although Hübner is a co-author.
  - — [arXiv:2408.10616](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10616v1)
- **Iigaya, Yi, Wahle, Tanwisuth & O'Doherty 2021** (Nature Human Behaviour) [peer-reviewed].
  - **Linear feature summation**: v_i = Σ_j w_j f_ij, with weights per participant.
  - The final 13-feature set includes the **vertical centre of mass of the largest segment**, hue contrast, brightness contrast, blur, segment size, width-height ratio and 4 high-level features (concreteness, dynamics, temperature, valence). **Symmetry features were candidates but were dropped by LASSO**, and balance is not a feature.
  - Out-of-sample prediction for online raters (N=1,359 on paintings): mean r = .46; low-level features alone r = .39. For photographs (AVA), r = .24.
  - In ImageNet-trained VGG-16, the decodability of the "vertical mass centre of the largest segment" *increased* with depth, while hue and saturation were decodable mainly in early layers.
  - — [Iigaya et al. 2021, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8494016)
- **Conwell, Graham, Boccagno & Vessel 2025** (PNAS, "The perceptual primacy of feeling") [peer-reviewed; details via a secondary summary]. Linear readouts from 180 networks trained only on vision tasks explain **a majority of the explainable variance** in average beauty, arousal and valence ratings. A related study found that unimodal vision models capture up to about 75% of the explainable variance in aesthetic ratings, more than language-aligned models [arXiv:2410.23603, preprint]. — [DOI 10.1073/pnas.2306025121](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2306025121); [secondary summary](https://mindblog.dericbownds.net/2025/02/the-perceptual-primacy-of-feeling.html); [arXiv:2410.23603](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.23603)
- **Explicit composition attributes are hard for CNNs** [preprint, arXiv 2017]. A multi-task CNN trained on eight aesthetic attributes "significantly underperformed for Rule of Thirds and Balancing elements". — [Learning Photography Aesthetics with Deep CNNs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03981)
- **CLIP and aesthetics** [peer-reviewed; Hentschel, Kobs & Hotho 2022, Frontiers in AI]. CLIP features suit image aesthetic assessment better than ImageNet features, possibly because language supervision captures composition and lighting. — [Frontiers in AI 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2022.976235/epub)
- **"Does CLIP perceive art the same way we do?"** [preprint, arXiv:2505.05229, 2025]. Probing against human annotations concludes that CLIP **lacks a robust internal model of aesthetic form**. It probes style, period and artifacts rather than balance specifically. — [arXiv:2505.05229](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.05229v2)
- **Interpretable concept-based assessment** [preprint, arXiv:2603.18108, 2026]. Using CLIP-ResNet50 concept bottlenecks, mid-level contributions come from structural attributes ("rule of thirds, balancing and symmetry"), and Composition is the dominant concept on the PARA dataset. — [arXiv:2603.18108](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.18108)

### Inferences
- **Abstract stimuli are out of distribution.** CNN/CLIP aesthetic predictors are fitted to photographs and paintings, where balance is confounded with content. Their good fit to *average* ratings (Conwell et al.; Iigaya et al.) does not imply sensitivity to pure CoM balance in abstract grids. The project's code-generated abstract stimuli are therefore the right test, and the Aesthetics Toolbox (or a local DCM/APB/HG implementation following Hübner & Fillinger 2016) can score them on the same scales used in human studies.
- **What the CNN evidence predicts for models.** Some sensitivity to *vertical* mass placement is plausible (Iigaya et al.'s deep-layer feature). Strong sensitivity to *left-right* balance is less certain, since explicit "balancing elements" was a weak attribute. A model that behaves as if following a textbook rule ("balanced compositions are better") may reflect language priors rather than visual encoding. Separating these needs stimuli where balance is varied but describing it verbally is hard.

### Gaps
- I found no study that linearly probes CLIP or VLM image embeddings for APB/DCM balance, inward facing, colour weight or golden-ratio proportion, and none applying these mathematical measures to *generated* images with human validation. This appears to be an open gap. (VLM evaluation methods were out of scope by assignment.)
- I could not confirm whether the Aesthetics Toolbox implements DCM, APB or homogeneity. Its GitHub README was not checked.
